        Behind the listening experience

        From a voice
        to a musical score.

        Real recordings enter the system. Signal analysis measures their phrases. Those measurements become a playable guide, which a generative model uses to compose music.

        01 / Input · Recorded

        Listen to the animal.

        Short excerpts of hydrophone recordings: humpback song, dolphin whistles, sperm-whale codas, and killer-whale calls.

        Audio + recording location
        02 / Schema · Measured

        Measure the phrase.

        Onsets, durations, click groups, and pitch contours are extracted from audio. Tonal calls and clicks have different representations.

        Events + time + frequency
        03 / Guide · Constructed

        Give it an instrument.

        Contours become tones and clicks become mallet strikes. Each player's instrument and register are combined with your trims, timing, and arrangement.

        A playable combined guide
        04 / Output · Generated

        Compose a response.

        ACE-Step 1.5 transforms the combined guide into music. A deterministic response provides a comparison that follows the measured events by construction.

        What is being tested?

        Does the music follow the phrase?

        Timing scores compare musical onsets with the animal's measured onsets, against the same output shifted in time. They help distinguish a response to this phrase from music that simply sounds appropriate.

        What is the artwork?

        A musical interpretation.

        The recordings are animal sounds. The measurements are signal analysis. The guide is a human-designed mapping and the composition is generated music. This process does not decode animal meaning.

        Development & models

        Tools, choices and authorship.

        We used Claude Code and OpenAI Codex to help develop and debug the software. Livia Zaharia and Anton Kulaga define the project concept, artistic direction and methodological choices, make the final decisions, and take responsibility for the work.

        Measuring the recordings. Signal analysis extracts call timing and pitch contours. Our code maps those measurements to instruments and registers, then combines them into a playable audio guide.

        Generating the music. We run ACE-Step 1.5's pretrained base checkpoint locally. Its diffusion transformer generates music from the combined audio guide and a caption. Our current pipeline leaves the model's optional language-model planning stage disabled.

        Checking the response. A deterministic musical response provides a comparison, and timing scores test how closely the generated music follows the measured events. Original recordings, constructed guides and generated music remain separately playable.

        Sound sources

        Where the voices come from.

        The prototype plays a few dozen excerpts, each under half a minute. They are a small subset, chosen by hand from open collections that hold years of recordings and many thousands of annotated calls. Each card says what we took.

        • Featured collection · PANGAEA

          Alfred Wegener Institute polar recordings

          Recorders on AWI moorings listen to the polar oceans year-round, and the Ocean Acoustics group publishes the recordings and their detection tables on PANGAEA. We take Antarctic blue whales from two moorings on the Greenwich meridian in the Weddell Sea, choosing each window from the published detections. Ilse Van Opzeeland, a co-author of both Weddell Sea recordings, works at AWI's Ocean Acoustics Lab and leads the Marine Acoustic Underwater Diversity (MAUD) group at the Helmholtz Institute for Functional Marine Biodiversity (HIFMB) in Oldenburg.

          Audio: Thomisch, Spiesecke, Lefering, Menze, Preis, Van Opzeeland & Boebel (2024), AWI232-11; Thomisch, Spiesecke, Van Opzeeland & Boebel (2024), AWI227-12. Detections: Thomisch, El-Gabbas, Spiesecke & Boebel (2023), AWI232-11 and AWI227-12.

        • NOAA · DCLDE workshops

          Annotated workshop datasets

          The DCLDE workshops publish recordings with analysts' labels for detection and classification research. 2027: killer whales and humpbacks from the Salish Sea, the outer coast and Alaska. 2013: right and fin whales at Stellwagen Bank. 2015: blue whales off the Channel Islands. 2022: minke whales, striped and rough-toothed dolphins on a Hawaiian survey. 2011: dolphin whistles traced at Palmyra Atoll and in the mid-Atlantic.

          Practical details

          What we need to build

          Stage

          The stage is arranged like an orchestra pit, with curved rows of chairs, music stands, species figures and miniature instruments. It has more than six places. Visitors select up to six chairs for each composition. The figures stay in place. LEDs mark the selected chairs, vary in brightness as each animal plays, and use colour to show its conservation status.

          Screen and sound

          One PC handles audio combination and music generation, playback and the display. Speakers play the orchestra in the room. The display shows spectrograms and follows playback in real time. Visitors select recording sites using wired buttons on a physical map, a touchscreen blackboard, or another interactive map display, depending on the chosen setup.

          For researchers

          This is a prototype idea. We'd be happy to extend and adjust it in collaboration with researchers, including during the ArtWaves residency at HIFMB. Some ideas we have:

          • Add recordings, check our phrase measurements against expert annotations, and explore how shipping noise masks calls with the Marine Acoustic Underwater Diversity group.
          • Use long-term recordings to show how a site's sound changes across seasons, with researchers in biodiversity theory and ecosystem data science.
          • Add protected areas and shipping lanes to the map with Marine Conservation and Marine Governance, and study visitors' responses to ocean sound with the qualitative research lab.
          • Improve the current method with researchers and musicians: compare bioacoustic detectors and music models, choose instruments and registers, and test how closely the music follows the measured calls.

          Related work includes Mirrors (2023), by Geraint Rhys Whittaker, Kimberley Peters and Ilse van Opzeeland, which follows a minke whale's acoustic journey and is described in their 2025 paper.

          A whale or dolphin recording reshapes Livia's ring. The preview is silent; press Play sound to listen at ¼ speed. Choose a ring and recording alongside the animation.

          Preparing the ring…

          The ring-bending study works with Livia's Inline and Roots models and checks how far each can move while remaining suitable for casting. This is an artistic mapping of measured sound; it does not decode animal meaning. The same method can be applied to other forms Livia designs.

          Planned editions. Website visitors will be able to choose a recording and download its sound-shaped STL. At installations, a small batch of 3D printed pieces made from the default sound combinations will be available to take or buy to support the research.

          A limited cast-silver edition is planned for visitors who donate to marine-science and conservation causes. Each installation will name the beneficiary and state the donation amount.

          About

          Architecture, nature
          and measured sound.

          Whale & Dolphin Orchestra grows from Livia Zaharia's practice as a Romanian architect, established parametric jewellery designer and science-art artist. It brings her work with computational form into marine sound and silver, in a pre-project prototype for the HIFMB × HWK ArtWaves residency. Anton Kulaga contributes bioinformatics, longevity research and open-source software.
